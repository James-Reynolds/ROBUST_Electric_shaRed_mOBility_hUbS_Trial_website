# Shared mobility
Shared mobility is recognised as having the potential to change travel behaviour and vehicle ownership patterns (EEA, 2016; Amatuni et al., 2020). Its strong growth in the market results from a gradual change in consumption patterns towards sharing economy of using more and owning less (Miramontes et al., 2017) and the emergence of new developments in information and communications technology (ICT) and other innovations. The key principle of shared mobility services is increased efficiency of the system which is delivered through better use of infrastructure and higher occupancy of vehicles (EEA, 2016). It offers a range of options from car-sharing, carpooling and bicycle sharing to on-demand rides which can aspire to replace car ownership.

Shared mobility provision offers several opportunities for transportation problems. First, it may reduce congestion and pollution by substituting car trips with smaller or/and electric vehicles (Bösehans et al., 2021). The literature shows that electric car-sharing replaced 27% of trips done previously by private cars (Martin & Shaheen, 2016). E-cargo bikes substituted the most significant percentage of private vehicle journeys among micro-mobility options – 46% (Becker & Rudolf, 2018). E-bike sharing had a more substantial effect on substituting private car use than traditional bike- sharing and replaced 17% of car journeys (Campbell et al., 2016). Emerging evidence on e-scooters shows even greater potential than e-bikes replacing 34% of those trips (Hollingsworth et al., 2019).

Simulations (Ciari & Becker, 2017) show that it is feasible to replace the majority of short private car trips (up to 5 km) with a combination of shared e-cars and e-bikes, and e-cars could be utilised for longer journeys (5–10 km). Moreover, there is also evidence of a positive effect of car-sharing on household vehicle ownership; for instance, Dutch service users reduced car ownership from 1.12 to 0.72 cars per household (Nijland & van Meerkerk, 2017). Second, shared mobility can also extend the catchment area of public transit by targeting the first and last mile of the trip (Shaheen & Chan, 2016). In particular, through integration with the public transit system, micro-vehicles have the potential to be an attractive alternative to private cars for longer distance trips (Oeschger, Carroll & Caulfield, 2020). Third, mobility sharing services can improve accessibility for places with poor transport connections and promote social equity for disadvantaged low-income groups underserved by existing transport provisions (MacArthur et al., 2017; Fleming, 2018). Fourth, shared mobility options can generate substantial health benefits, in particular when they substitute trips done previously by private cars (Otero, et al., 2018). Here, these benefits may be produced by bicycles but also by electrically assisted bikes (pedelecs), as these require certain amount of physical activity and belong in the active modes category (EEA, 2016). The simulations for Dublin show that shared mobility services have the potential to replace 20% of private car trips, which translates into an emissions reduction of more than 20%. This reduction does not assume that vehicles are electric; thus, supplying electric shared mobility options could cut emissions further (ITF, 2018). Another study shows that the introduction of shared cars in Dublin, assuming a certain level of uptake and that all vehicles would be electric, could save 84kt of CO2 annually (Rabbitt & Ghosh, 2016). The operation of the Yuko scheme in Dublin was found to decrease emissions and suppress car ownership (Caulfield & Kehoe, 2021). Assuming the rate of one shared car replacing up to 15 private cars (Millard-Ball et al., 2005), the reduction of 10 thousand cars could be observed in Dublin. If the shift could be achieved, it would also mean lower demand for the street space. The average private car is in active use only 3 to 4% of the time, and the remaining time occupies space parked at home or elsewhere (GOI, 2021a). Fewer car parks mean allocating more space to active travel modes and green places, enhancing the liveability of urban areas.

# Electric mobility vehicles
The most common vehicles being provided under shared mobility schemes are e-cars, e-bikes, e-cargo bikes, e-scooters and bicycles. Electric car-sharing allows users to access the benefits of a private vehicle without bearing the costs and responsibilities of ownership. Commuters are generally attracted to electric cars by their positive environmental aspects and low fuel costs (Weldon, Morrissey & O'Mahony, 2018). Several EU funded research projects focus on promoting electric vehicles; for instance, the TIDE project addresses awareness of electric mobility options and the Green eMotion project (on which members of the proposal team have worked) targets, among others, behavioural changes required to increase EV market penetration. Electric micro-mobility refers to light vehicles which are designed for short distances (up to 15 km). Micro-mobility can be a strong alternative to private cars for trips ranging from 2 to 5 km and, due to the compact size, be attractive during congested periods. Among micro-vehicles, e-bikes, e-scooters, and e-cargo bikes are the most popular. E-bikes can be classified into pedelecs, which require pedal assistance, and e-mopeds, which do not (Liao & Correia, 2022). The growing popularity is observed for e-scooters and e-cargo bikes among families who use them as family cars (Thomas, 2021).

Another option widely available is a push-bike or bicycle. It enables active travel and comes with substantial health benefits. A few studies have been published on bike-sharing in Dublin. Studies examining the use of Dublin Bikes indicate the scheme's success resulting in a more cycling-friendly city (O'Tuma, 2015) and a positive cost-benefit ratio under several different scenarios, with the lowest being 6.4:1 (Bullock et al., 2017). Another study of the Dublin Bikes scheme (Murphy & Usher, 2015) reports a significant modal shift showing that 20% of users would have driven the trip if the bikes had not been available. Moreover, recent research on the Yuko scheme in Dublin found that the scheme was suppressing car ownership (Caulfield & Kehoe, 2021).

# Mobility hubs
Shared mobility hubs offer integrated multimodal shared mobility services and facilitate intermodal transfers by providing various mobility options in close proximity (Miramontes et al., 2017). What distinguishes them from free-floating car-sharing and bike-sharing systems is the specific geographical location – a hub – which makes multimodal trips more convenient and creates a sense of designated shopping mall) or city/town centre around transit nodes. Hubs situated near public transport nodes offer the advantage of covering the first and last mile of the trip, hence expanding public transport coverage (Shaheen & Chan, 2016). A fixed site can provide a convenient location for installing charging infrastructure where shared electric vehicles can be recharged while being parked (Liao & Correia, 2022). A hub might also function as an activity centre with installed outdoor furniture and other nearby facilities. Most of the existing shared mobility hubs deploy e-cars and electric micro-mobility options, such as e-bikes, e-cargo bikes, and e-scooters. Fossil fuel car-sharing or bicycle-sharing may also be available (Liao & Correia, 2022), as well as recent technological prototypes, e.g. a two-seat quadricycle (Sopjani et al., 2020) or battery-powered attachment for wheelchairs (Bird Rides, 2022).

Since 2003, when Bremen launched Mobil.Punkt stations with car-sharing and bicycle parking spots situated next to public transport stops (Liao & Correia, 2022), several regions in Europe and beyond started to install multimodal mobility hubs, proving early evidence of their effectiveness in addressing transport problems. The solution is emerging but has proven attractive to its users (Frank et al., 2021). The initiative of Austin's Community Mobility Hub (Texas, USA) was set up and evaluated over two years, and the results indicate more frequent use of sustainable modes and a decrease in emissions (Rocky Mountain Institute, 2018). Several pilots are in progress. The MOBI-MIX pilots in the urban environment of Rotterdam, Norfolk, Antwerp, Mechelen and Valenciennes were founded by the European Regional Development Fund and run from 2020 to 2022 (Polis, 2022). eHUBS (Smart Shared Green Mobility Hubs) project was launched by the city of Amsterdam and partners in 6 pilot cities (Amsterdam, Manchester, Leuven, Arnhem, Kempten and Dreux) from 5 different countries and will run from 2019 to 2022 (Interreg North-West Europe, 2022). SMARTA mobihubs and hubtaxis were introduced in a rural area of Groningen Drenthe, the Netherlands (SMARTA, 2022). Early findings from the trials include discussion on determining the location and profiles of early shared mobility adopters. For instance, Bösehans et al. (2021) analysed potential user profiles of eHUBs in Amsterdam revealing high adoption intentions if the barriers were to be removed. Possible effects of mobility hubs were also examined for The Hague (Claasen, 2020). Despite the proven attractiveness of the hubs, the evidence on principles for successful deployment is still limited.

A few initiatives were recently undertaken in Ireland to establish smart mobility hubs. In 2019, Dublin City Council, in partnership with Enterprise, launched a shared workplace "mobility hub" for staff at Wood Quay Civic Offices (Dublin City, 2020). It aimed to encourage employees to commute to work by public transport and access shared e-cars, e-bikes and bicycles for business travel. The first public mobility hub was launched by Fingal County Council in Blanchardstown a year after (Fingal County Council, 2020). The hub comprises five parking bays with electric vehicle charging and offers bike-sharing and car-sharing services. Under the TRACT project, the mobility hub will be established in a suburban location in Dublin, giving the team on the ROBUST project an opportunity to engage and exchange knowledge, building capacity for large-scale implementation of shared mobility options.
